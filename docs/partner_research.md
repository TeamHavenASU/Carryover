# Partner research and Insights: First Place AZ

## Research goal

Find out where autistic adults at First Place get stuck when they plan, start or work through daily tasks, and what support they have now.

We wanted to learn three things:

1. **Where the hard moments are.** Which tasks, or which parts of a task, cause the most trouble?
2. **What they do now.** How are skills taught today, and where does that fall short?
3. **What good support looks like.** What would make a tool helpful, and what would make people stop using it?

## Who we talked to and when

We talked with First Place staff, not residents. We did not collect any resident data.

| Date | Session | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Nov 3 | Meeting with staff | We asked how skills are taught now and what would help |
| Nov 4 | Meeting with staff | We showed our design and asked for feedback |

Everything below is from our notes and is paraphrased. There are no direct quotes.

**How our direction developed.** After the site visit we had a clearer idea of what residents and staff deal with day to day, and started researching from there. We considered a few ideas first and dropped the ones where good apps already exist. That led us to our topic: help with daily living skills that adapts to each person. The Nov 3 meeting confirmed this direction, and we made changes based on it. On Nov 4 we showed staff our design and changed it based on their feedback.

## What we heard

**Site visit (Oct 27)**
- About 75 adults live there, 18 and older, with no maximum age. There is also a Transition Academy.
- It can take 2–3 months for someone new to settle in and start doing tasks.
- Most people live there 2–3 years and then move out, sometimes to a place nearby.
- It is in central Phoenix, partly so help comes fast in a medical emergency or from support staff.
- Driving is not taught, and it is one of the big barriers to independence.
- Residents learn to cook in a teaching kitchen that has the same stoves as their rooms.
- Labels on every cupboard show what's inside. Each floor has its own colors, so residents can tell floors apart.
- Sound systems soften sound so people don't get overwhelmed.
- There is a spot by the door to keep things so they aren't forgotten.
- The library's self-checkout has its own instructions and pictures. Every room has an Alexa.
- The stoves in the rooms turn off by themselves if nothing happens for a while. The person from the company that makes the device said it has been used many times. When we asked, staff said there is nothing like this for the microwave.
- Staff use online curricula and task analyses, like AFLS, and a video library made by a university for dating and social skills.
- A staff member said help with building skills and keeping progress going without much staff help would be useful.

**Meeting with staff (Nov 3)**
- Help that adapts to each person, for independent living skills, would be useful.
- The way tasks are taught now is normative: the same steps for everyone.
- They suggested using task analysis, and doing a literature review.
- When we asked about tools for money, they mentioned options like Greenlight cards, which might be more restrictive, and spreadsheets, which might be less restrictive.

**Feedback on our design (Nov 4)**
- The steps and the wait time before help is offered are fine.
- Show cautions without having to tap.
- A picture and a video of how each step is done would help.
- If it feels formal, people may not use it. Make it feel more like a game.
- We said our research found surprises can bother some people. They suggested asking each person's preferences first and going from there.
- Problem solving is a big hurdle. Clear steps don't help if someone doesn't have the basic skill a step assumes.
- A new or different device, like a different microwave, is hard.
- Some people need much more detail. For example, making a peanut butter sandwich: which hand holds the jar, and which way to turn the lid.
- Skills learned at First Place should carry over to living on their own.

## What we think it means

Each row goes from a pattern we noticed, to what residents need, to the rule our design has to follow.

| Pattern | What residents need | Design rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teaching uses the same steps for everyone, but people differ a lot in how much help they need and where | Help that changes per person and per step, and gets smaller as they learn | Keep the task analysis staff already use; change only how much help each person sees |
| Residents already rely on labels, pictures and memory aids around the building | Support that fits the cues they already use | Short text and a picture on each step, with the same layout every time |
| Safety supports exist for the stove but not the microwave, and staff want cautions seen without tapping | Safety information that can't be missed | Cautions always visible, at every help level |
| A new device, a missing basic skill, or a step that needs more detail can stop someone | Help with what a step takes for granted | Ask about new devices; offer skill cards; allow more detailed steps |
| People differ in what they like and what bothers them | A tool set up around each person | Ask preferences first, then follow them |
| First Place is a stage: people leave after a few years, and staff want progress without much staff help | A way to learn skills without staff nearby | The resident uses the app on their own, and help fades as they learn |

## How the research changed Carryover

Because staff told us teaching is the same for everyone and that help adapting to each person would be useful, we built Carryover around help that changes per person and per step and fades as they learn, while keeping the same task analysis staff already use.

Other changes. The last column shows which session each one came from:

| What we heard | What we changed | From |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same steps for everyone; adaptive help would be useful | Each person sees a different amount of help on each step. Help fades after they do a step alone and comes back if they struggle | Nov 3 |
| Use task analysis and a lit review | Every task is a task analysis in our own words (no AFLS text). Our design choices are backed by research | Nov 3 |
| Progress without much staff help | The resident uses the app on their own. Sharing a summary with staff is the resident's choice and off by default (the demo's Staff page shows all simulated residents) | Oct 27 |
| Show cautions without tapping | Caution lines always show, at every help level | Nov 4 |
| A different microwave is hard | The first time, the app asks "Is this a microwave you've used before?" and records the answer. The rule to start at the most help when the answer is no is written and tested, but not yet connected in the app | Nov 4 |
| Missing basic skills | Each step lists the skills it needs. If someone isn't sure, a short skill card shows first | Nov 4 |
| Much more detail for some people | On the Staff page, "Ask AI for more detail" breaks a step down further (which hand, which way). A person approves the new steps before anyone sees them | Nov 4 |
| Pictures and video | Each step has a picture. Videos are planned | Nov 4 |
| Make it feel like a game | Optional Game mode: the person chooses a world (sky, animals or space), picks a reward after each task, and new areas open as they go. Rewards never depend on using less help | Nov 4 |
| Ask preferences first, then go from there | A short first-time setup (which world they like, Game or Normal, sound volume, which hand) that can be skipped. The app follows those choices, and a "Tell us what you think" section in Settings asks about movement, sound, colors and amount of help | Nov 4 |
| Skills should carry over to living on their own | The app works without staff, and help fades as people learn, so they depend on it less over time | Nov 4 |
| The steps and wait time are fine | Kept the steps. After testing the app ourselves, the first help offer now comes a little sooner (about 7 seconds on most steps) | Nov 4 |
