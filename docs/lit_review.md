# Literature Review: Supporting Autistic Adults in Learning Daily Living Tasks

## 1. Introduction

Many autistic adults want to live more independently, and everyday tasks like heating a meal or paying in a store are part of that. These tasks can be hard for reasons that have little to do with how much a person knows. Starting a task, remembering the next step, or coping when a device looks different can all get in the way (Hill, 2004a; Buckle et al., 2021). Support also has a cost of its own: help that never goes away can leave people depending on it.

This review asks two questions:

1. What does research say about how to support autistic adults in learning daily living tasks?
2. Where does a tool like Carryover fit, and what is still unknown?

Carryover is an app that shows a task one step at a time, learns where each person gets stuck on each step, shows the smallest help that works, and reduces that help as the person learns. This review explains the research behind that idea, where the research is strong, and where it is thin.

To answer these questions, we searched Google Scholar, PubMed and journal websites, and followed the reference lists of key papers. Each study was checked against its abstract or full text, and we note when participants were children or people with intellectual disability rather than autistic adults.

The review is organized by theme. It looks first at why daily tasks are hard, then at how these skills are taught, how technology and video can support them, and what tools already exist. It then turns to choice and motivation, screen design, and the reliability of AI, before setting out the gaps that remain. Official guidance, such as USDA food safety rules and payment help pages from Apple and Google, was used to write the task steps but is not reviewed here, and what our partner First Place AZ told us is reported in our partner research document. Full references are in `docs/sources.md`.

## 2. Why daily tasks can be hard

Executive function is the set of thinking skills that let a person plan, hold information in mind, switch between tasks, start and stop actions, and check their own work (Hill, 2004a). Miyake et al. (2000) showed that these skills are related but separate, with three core parts: updating what is held in mind, shifting between tasks, and holding back an automatic response. This matters for daily tasks, because heating a meal draws on all of them at once.

A meta-analysis of 235 studies found a broad, moderate difference in executive function between autistic and non-autistic people, and the difference was similar across ages (Demetriou et al., 2018). Studies with adults point the same way. Johnston et al. (2019) compared 110 autistic adults with 31 matched controls and found differences both on executive function tasks and in everyday functioning, and the autistic adults often reported dysexecutive behaviours that were disabling in everyday life. Yon-Hernández et al. (2023) found that executive function predicted daily living skills in autistic adults, and that people's own reports predicted more than lab tasks did. The authors suggest that planning and starting actions carry much of the load in daily tasks.

The evidence also shows how much people differ. In Johnston et al. (2019), 35.8% of the autistic group showed no impairment on executive function measures, and Hill (2004b) notes that results across studies are mixed. Executive function difficulties are common in autistic adults, but not universal. A tool that assumes every user struggles in the same place would be wrong for many of them.

Starting a task deserves special attention. Buckle et al. (2021), in a study led by an autistic researcher, held focus groups with 32 autistic adults about "autistic inertia." Participants described difficulty starting, stopping and changing activities, including trouble finding the first step, and said that prompting and the right activity around them helped them act. Lab studies support this. Carmo et al. (2015) found signs of difficulty starting responses in a word-listing test, and a follow-up study found that simple outside verbal cues helped people get past it (Carmo et al., 2017). These are lab tests rather than daily tasks, and the 2015 authors call their results preliminary, but together with Buckle et al. they suggest that a short cue or a clear first step can help someone begin.

## 3. How daily living skills are taught

### Task analysis, prompting and fading

The most common way to teach a daily task is task analysis: breaking the task into small, ordered steps and teaching each one. Steps are usually taught with prompting, which means giving help such as a picture, a model or a hint, and then fading that help as the person learns. A large review of intervention research for autistic children, youth and young adults identified 28 evidence-based practices, including prompting, task analysis, time delay, video modeling, technology-aided instruction and self-management (Hume et al., 2021). MacDuff et al. (2001) describe how prompts can be faded, for example by giving less help over time, stepping back to more help after errors, and waiting before helping so the person has a chance to act alone (time delay).

Published task analyses exist for the tasks Carryover covers. For microwave cooking, Smith (1995) used a general case approach that teaches across different microwaves, whose buttons and labels differ. Sigafoos et al. (2005) taught three adults with developmental disabilities to use a microwave with video prompts on a computer. Jones and Collins (1997) found that adults with disabilities learned nutrition and safety facts that were placed inside the instructions for microwave tasks, even though those facts were not the main target. For buying, Rowe et al. (2011) used classroom practice with picture prompts to teach three students with disabilities to pay with a debit card and track their spending. All three kept the skill for four weeks and used it at three community sites. Adams et al. (2026) taught three adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities a decision strategy for buying online, CAN-B, that asks about cost, whether they can afford it, whether they need it, and which option is best.

How many steps should a task have? Barnard-Brak et al. (2023) offer initial guidance for people with intellectual disabilities: about seven steps or fewer, written at about a seventh-grade reading level. The authors present this as a starting point, not a firm rule.

### Mastery and keeping skills

A key question is when to reduce help. In a survey of practitioners, Richling et al. (2019) found that the most common rule is 80% correct across three sessions in a row. In three experiments with four children with developmental disabilities, that rule was not always enough for the skill to last. Fuller and Fienup (2018) taught three autistic children with 80% and 90% mastery rules, and the 90% rule led to the best maintenance. Both studies are small and with children, but they suggest two things: common mastery rules are a reasonable starting point, and skills can fade after learning, so a check after a long break is sensible.

### Assessments behind task analysis

Teaching programs often start with an assessment of which skills a person has. The Assessment of Functional Living Skills (AFLS; Partington & Mueller, 2012) is an assessment, skills tracking system and curriculum guide covering hundreds of daily living skills, with task analyses and prompting strategies, for children through adults. First Place AZ staff told us they use AFLS alongside other curricula. Carryover's tasks follow the same task analysis method, but all steps are written in our own words. No AFLS text was used.

### Where these methods come from, and what autistic adults say about them

Task analysis, prompting, time delay and reinforcement come from applied behavior analysis (ABA). ABA is widely used with autistic children, and it is also widely criticized by autistic adults, especially for focusing on compliance, on making autistic people appear non-autistic (masking), and on outside rewards (Baiden et al., 2025). Two recent surveys asked autistic adults directly. Baiden et al. (2025) surveyed 235 autistic adults and found that goals centred on safety and quality of life, such as increasing independence, were seen as acceptable, while goals aimed at making people seem "normal" were not. Participants preferred natural rewards over outside rewards, learning in real settings, and using the person's own interests. Chazin et al. (2025) surveyed 660 people, 226 of them autistic, and found high acceptance for goals that promote self-determination and low acceptance for goals that promote masking. Both surveys asked about practices used with children, but they show what autistic adults see as acceptable.

This shapes how Carryover uses these methods. It applies task analysis and fading to practical skills the person chooses, in the real setting where the task happens. It asks before reducing help, uses no points or rewards in its normal mode, and does not try to change how a person behaves beyond the task itself. Carryover is not an ABA program, and we describe it as prompt fading.

## 4. Technology, video, and what already exists

### Video and phones

Video is one of the best-supported ways to prompt daily tasks. A meta-analysis of 17 single-case studies with 54 participants found a moderate effect of video prompting on daily living skills for autistic people (Aljehany & Bennett, 2019). A review of 24 single-case studies with 64 people with intellectual disability found that video prompting and modeling on phones and tablets were effective, mostly for household tasks (Stierle et al., 2023). Clips filmed from the person's own point of view showed larger to very large effects (Wertalik et al., 2025). Cannella-Malone et al. (2006) compared video prompting, where each of 10 steps has its own short clip, with video modeling, where one video shows the whole task, when teaching six adults with developmental disabilities to set a table and put away groceries. Video prompting led to fast learning in all but one case, while video modeling was generally ineffective. Narration is optional: video prompting worked with or without voice-over instructions (Dawson et al., 2023).

These studies support short, step-by-step media on a phone. Most participants were children or people with intellectual disability, and Stierle et al. (2023) found no studies on personal care tasks such as dressing or grooming.

### Tools that already exist

Several tools already support daily living. Autominder gives adaptive reminders for people with memory impairment, tracking the person's plan rather than using fixed alarms (Pollack et al., 2003). It helps with when to do a task, not with how to do it. Brain in Hand combines an app with human support for autistic adults. In a 12-week before-and-after study in which 99 adults started and 66 finished, anxiety fell and daily functioning improved (Tromans et al., 2023). There was no control group, three authors worked for the company, and the study was funded by a grant to the company, although the authors state the research was independent. Its focus is anxiety and planning the day rather than learning a single task.

Commercial step-by-step tools are closer to Carryover. AbleLink's Visual Impact lets staff or caregivers build step-by-step tasks with photos, video and audio, which the person then uses on their own; its Endeavor app links scheduled reminders to those tasks, and its WayFinder app guides people with intellectual disabilities when traveling by bus or on foot (AbleLink Smart Living Technologies, n.d.). Avail (CentralReach, n.d.) provides individualized prompts through task analysis, with more than 100 ready-made tasks that staff can customize, and progress reports. MeMinder (CreateAbility, n.d.) describes using prompt fading, moving from detailed video prompts toward minimal text or audio, with caregivers or support staff adjusting each task and using progress tracking to decide when someone is ready for less. Staff-facing data tools also record prompt levels. In one, Rethink, staff change the prompt level manually once the mastery criteria are met (Rethink, n.d.).

In every tool we found, help is set by a person, and none of the product pages describe help that adapts to where each person gets stuck and fades on that step alone. These products may do more than their pages describe, so this is a statement about what we could find, not about everything that exists. Carryover does not cover travel; a tool like WayFinder could be used alongside it.

## 5. Choice, motivation, and learning on one's own terms

### Self-determination

Self-determination research is central to the design. Wehmeyer and Abery (2013) describe choice and control as core parts of self-determination. Wehmeyer (1999) presents self-determination as a characteristic of the person and a model for designing instruction, and Causal Agency Theory extends that model, focusing on support that helps people act for themselves through setting and reaching their own goals (Shogren et al., 2015). Wehmeyer (2020) links self-determination to quality of life for people with intellectual disability. A review by Burke et al. (2024) summarizes this research, including the Self-Determination Inventory and the Self-Determined Learning Model of Instruction, a model in which learners set goals, plan and check their own progress. That review focuses on students with intellectual and developmental disabilities moving from school to adult life, so it supports the general direction more than any specific feature.

Setting also matters. Wehmeyer and Bolding (2001) found that adults with intellectual disability scored higher on self-determination, autonomy and life choices about six months after moving from a more restrictive work or living setting to a community-based one. This was a before-and-after study with no control group. Communities like First Place AZ are community settings, and that is our own reasoning; the study did not test an app or autistic adults.

### Learning on one's own

Self-management, in which people track and guide their own behaviour, is one of the evidence-based practices identified by Hume et al. (2021), and the video studies above show that a phone can prompt a person without a staff member beside them (Aljehany & Bennett, 2019; Stierle et al., 2023). Together with self-determination research, this supports a tool that the person uses on their own, that lets them decide when help is reduced, and that fades so they rely on it less over time. Most of this evidence comes from students and people with intellectual disability, so it supports the idea rather than proving it for autistic adults.

### Rewards, praise and interests

Rewards need care. A meta-analysis of 128 studies found that expected, tangible rewards lowered people's own interest in an activity, while positive feedback raised it (Deci et al., 1999). The studies were mostly with children and college students, not autistic adults. Praise style also varies by person: three adults with autism and intellectual disability learned slightly faster with enthusiastic praise, but only one preferred it, while the other two showed no clear preference (Weyman & Sy, 2018). These findings support short, factual feedback, and letting the person choose how praise sounds.

Interests offer a different kind of motivation. About two-thirds of autistic adults in one study had a special interest, and most had more than one; stronger motivation for the interest went with higher wellbeing, while very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing (Grove et al., 2018). Autistic adults also endorse using a person's interests to teach skills (Baiden et al., 2025). In a small trial with 24 autistic adults over five weeks, a gamified physical activity app was used longer than a standard app, though other results were mixed (Lee et al., 2022), and its authors note that rigorous studies of gamification with autistic people are few (Lee et al., 2025). The evidence for games is thin and points both ways. This is why Carryover's Game mode is optional, built around the person's interests, and never rewards using less help.

## 6. Screen design for autistic users

Accessibility guidance for people with cognitive disabilities recommends clear steps, a consistent layout, not relying on memory, and easy access to human help (W3C, n.d.), and autism-specific guidelines built from a survey of 398 autistic people add literal language, soft colours and no autoplay (Hassell Inclusion & National Autistic Society, 2026). Research points the same way: nearly all of 170 autistic users found a co-designed website easy to use (Raymaker et al., 2019), while autistic adolescents did worse on richer screens that combined text, speech, images and animated agents (Grynszpan et al., 2008). This supports simple screens with one step at a time, designed with autistic users. Carryover's full screen rules and their sources are in `docs/ui_guidelines.md`.

## 7. Reliability of AI

Carryover uses an AI model only for open-ended questions, such as asking for help with a photo, and research shows why the model's own confidence cannot be trusted. Language models are overconfident when asked how sure they are, and checking whether repeated answers agree works better (Xiong et al., 2024); vision-language models can name objects that are not in an image (Li et al., 2023); and declining to answer when unsure keeps errors low (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017). This is why Carryover's AI must quote the label it reads, asks three times, and hands off to a person when unsure. The full rules and our test results are in `docs/responsible_ai.md`.

## 8. Gaps in the research

Several gaps stand out.

First, we found no studies of app-based or AI-driven prompt fading for autistic adults. Prompt fading is well supported when trainers do it (MacDuff et al., 2001; Hume et al., 2021), and in the tools we found, a person decides when to reduce help. Whether fading can be done well by an app that learns from each person's use is untested.

Second, much of the evidence comes from children, from people with intellectual disability, or from small single-case studies. The major review of evidence-based practices covers children, youth and young adults (Hume et al., 2021), the mastery studies were with children (Richling et al., 2019; Fuller & Fienup, 2018), and the video reviews include many participants with intellectual disability (Stierle et al., 2023). Several executive function findings come from lab tasks rather than real kitchens or stores (Carmo et al., 2015, 2017; Hill, 2004b).

Third, the exact numbers in Carryover, such as reducing help after three successful sessions or offering a refresher after a long break, are supported by research on the idea but not on the exact values.

Fourth, evidence on games with autistic adults is thin and mixed (Lee et al., 2022; Deci et al., 1999).

Fifth, autistic adults' own voices are still missing from much of this research, and from ours so far: our partner research was with First Place staff, not residents. Guidelines for including autistic adults as co-researchers and participants exist (Nicolaidis et al., 2019), and following them is the next step.

These gaps are the reason our next step is a pilot with First Place residents who choose to take part, described in `docs/future_directions.md`.

## 10. Conclusion

Research gives a clear picture of how daily living skills are taught: break the task into steps, give the smallest help that works, and fade it as the person learns. It also shows that autistic adults differ a great deal in where they get stuck, that starting a task can be a particular barrier, and that short step-by-step prompts on a phone can work. Autistic adults themselves favour goals that build independence and self-determination, natural rather than outside rewards, and the use of their own interests.

What the research does not yet show is whether this kind of help can be adapted and faded well by an app, for each person and each step, with autistic adults rather than children or trainers. Existing tools leave that decision to staff. Carryover is built to test that idea carefully: it keeps the person in control, fades help without hiding safety information, and keeps a person involved wherever AI could be wrong. The next step is to test it with the people it is for.
